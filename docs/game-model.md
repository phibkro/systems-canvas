# Game Model

**Why do agents choose the actions they do, given their interests, information, available choices and rules?**

A Game Model represents an **action situation**: a bounded strategic interaction within or between enterprises. Game here refers to game theory, not gamification; it does not imply all actors are adversaries, selfish, or exclusively motivated by money.

## An action situation

| Construct | Core question |
|---|---|
| Participants / agents | Who can take consequential actions? |
| Positions & roles | Who acts in which capacity, including principal, delegate, worker or regulator? |
| Actions & strategies | What options exist? What sequences or conditional strategies are possible? |
| Rules & authority | Which actions are permitted, required, constrained or sanctionable? |
| Information | Who observes which signals, when, and with what uncertainty? |
| Preferences & incentives | What does each participant value? Which rewards, costs or constraints matter? |
| Interaction & outcomes | How do choices combine to affect all participants and other stakeholders? |
| Revision | Who can change the rules of this game, and who governs that decision? |

A **stakeholder** may be affected without being a player. Conversely, an AI agent may act for a human or organizational principal without sharing the principal's welfare interests.

## Agency, autonomy and governance

Do not equate being a participant with being autonomous or empowered. Record which actions an agent **can perform**, **can initiate**, **may legitimately perform**, and **can practically access** given its resources and information; locate these claims in an action situation and time. Separate discretion over operations, authority to select goals and authority to revise the governing rules. A persistent AI workflow is not necessarily an autonomous agent. [Shared semantic definitions](/docs/system-ontology-and-taxonomy/) prevent these distinctions from becoming inconsistent across enterprise and game views.

When interaction outcomes feed back into regulation, learning or institutional evolution, open the [Dynamic/Cybernetic Model](/docs/dynamic-cybernetic-model/).

## Progressive formalization

1. **Qualitative:** Record participants, positions, choices, rules, possible outcomes, conflicts and evidence. This is the default.
2. **Structured:** Specify information, timing, constraints, dependencies, alternative explanations and stakeholder-relative consequences.
3. **Formal (optional):** Define strategic and payoff structures and analyze possible equilibria only when assumptions and evidence justify that level of precision.

Ostrom's IAD action-situation framework is an important practical foundation. Formal and compositional game theory can be added as specialized tools, not assumed prerequisites.

## Example: journalism and platform distribution

A newsroom may have the capacity to produce investigations, but short-term visibility metrics, platform ranking changes, editorial authority, time constraints and participant incentives might affect what gets published. These are **hypotheses** to investigate, not factual claims about a particular platform.

- The **Enterprise Model** shows reporting capacity, production, distribution and governance.
- The **Game Model** asks who can choose what, which outcomes they anticipate, what information they have, and whose goals conflict.
- A **revision** could change capacity, decision rights, incentives or measurement—and must include a test for unintended effects.

The enterprise shapes the game; the game's outcomes may change enterprise structure, rules and subsequent games.

## Institutional terminology

**Institutional analysis** studies rules-in-use, positions, collective action and governance arrangements. This differs from the formal **institution theory** used in mathematical logic and specification frameworks. Both may eventually inform software representations, but they are not interchangeable.
