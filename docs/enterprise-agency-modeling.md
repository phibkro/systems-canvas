# Enterprise & Agency Modeling

**Proposed modeling framework · open research · not yet empirically validated**

Enterprise & Agency Modeling is a way to investigate **how purposeful enterprises operate, why participating agents behave as they do, and how changing structures and incentives might improve outcomes**. It combines two complementary model families with an evidence-oriented method: **frame → model → diagnose → evaluate → revise → test and monitor**.

An *enterprise* is a purposeful undertaking realized through interacting agents, capabilities, resources, rules and activities. It may span businesses, cooperatives, public institutions, civic initiatives, partnerships, or human–AI workflows. **An enterprise is not synonymous with a company or a legal organization.** Boundaries are selected for the question being investigated.

## Two linked models

| | Enterprise Model | Game Model |
|---|---|---|
| Primary question | **How** is the undertaking organized and regulated? | **Why** might participants choose particular actions? |
| Main constructs | Actors, capabilities, resources, processes, authority, information, feedback, outcomes | Participants, positions, actions, information, rules, preferences, incentives, strategies, outcomes |
| Unit of analysis | A bounded undertaking and its recursive organization | A bounded action situation or strategic interaction |
| Typical diagnoses | Coordination failure, bottlenecks, missing feedback, governance gaps | Perverse incentives, misaligned objectives, asymmetric information, coordination failures |
| Possible revisions | Reallocate capacity, change processes or decision rights, improve monitoring | Change rules, information, choices, accountability, or payoff structures |

[Explore the Enterprise Model](/docs/enterprise-model/) · [Explore the Game Model](/docs/game-model/) · [Try the interactive canvas](/canvas/)

The two models **share identities, rules, observations and outcome claims**; they should not become incompatible databases. Enterprise structures shape action situations; strategic decisions can reproduce or change those structures. One enterprise can contain multiple nested games, and a game can involve participants from multiple organizations.

## Shared viewpoints

Our six lenses are *projections*, not six separate models:

| Lens | Enterprise questions | Game questions |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Who participates? What capabilities and dependencies exist? | Who occupies which position? |
| Behavior | What processes, flows, delays and feedback recur? | What actions and strategies are available? |
| Knowledge | What can be observed or supported by evidence? | Who knows what when choices are made? |
| Authority | Who sets or enforces rules, allocates resources and hears appeals? | Which rules constrain each player's choices? |
| Purpose | Which ends are declared, contested or operationalized? | Whose preferences and objectives shape decisions? |
| Outcomes | What value, harms and externalities occur, for whom? | How are the consequences and payoffs distributed? |

**A view is not evidence.** Causal arrows and predicted choices remain hypotheses until investigated. Keep provenance, competing explanations and uncertainty explicit.

## Prescribed practice

1. **Frame:** State a concrete outcome, participants affected, boundary, time horizon and evaluation criteria.
2. **Model:** Begin with the smallest sufficient enterprise picture; introduce an action-situation/game model when strategic behavior could explain the outcome.
3. **Diagnose:** Identify mechanisms, competing explanations, observations, incentives and possible feedback loops.
4. **Evaluate:** Assess effectiveness, viability, legitimacy, distributional effects and reliability of the evidence.
5. **Revise:** Identify authorized changes to capabilities, processes, rules, information or incentives. Compare alternatives.
6. **Test and monitor:** Define baseline, safeguards, counterfactual or comparison where feasible, stopping criteria and revision responsibilities.

The method is iterative, not a waterfall. Complexity should be revealed only when it materially changes the explanation, evaluation or decision.

## What about agents?

**Agent** denotes a modeled actor capable of relevant action or choice; it does not imply that every actor is a human or an autonomous AI. Keep *agent*, *affected stakeholder*, *principal* and *governor* distinct roles. A worker can be both an agent and an affected stakeholder. An AI agent can act under delegated permissions without automatically having the moral interests of a person.

This supports analyzing **human, mixed human–AI and software-agent enterprises** without treating AI agents as equivalent to humans.

## Relationship to Human Viable Systems

[Human Viable Systems](/docs/human-viable-systems/) is the related **normative research program** asking which forms of organizational viability support human autonomy, welfare, legitimate governance and ecological sustainability. Enterprise & Agency Modeling is the **descriptive and intervention-oriented approach**, usable with HVS evaluation or with other explicitly declared values.

**Viability is not desirability.** A system can be stable and effective at producing outcomes that harm people or ecosystems. The analyst must separate observed performance from chosen evaluation criteria.

## Intellectual foundations and limits

We reuse rather than replace established work: Stafford Beer's VSM, Raúl Espejo's Enterprise Complexity Model, Ostrom's IAD action situations, 4EM/EKD, enterprise architecture, system dynamics, cybernetics and game theory. Formal open/compositional games are optional deeper representations; an introductory Game Model can be qualitative. These distinctions are a proposed integration and teaching interface, **not** a claim of a new general theory or demonstrated diagnostic superiority.
